Lesson 0001

Events, Behavior, Structure

Why the same incident keeps coming back no matter who is on shift.

Today's win: classify any claim about a system as an event, a behavior over time, or a structure, and spot when a fix only touches the top level.

The central claim

The system, to a large extent, causes its own behavior. Donella Meadows, Thinking in Systems (2008)

This is a testable claim. Put the same people, competence and intentions into a different structure and you get different behavior. Keep the structure, swap the people, and the behavior mostly comes back.

You saw this on the hangar floor. A repeat defect on one fleet type survived every shift and every technician. Something in the setup kept producing it: the task card, parts supply, inspection interval, or the pressure to release on time. Retraining or reprimanding the individual changed nothing, because the individual wasn't the cause. Senge's last law says the same thing: "there is no blame." [Senge]

Three levels of seeing

In chapter 4, "Why Systems Surprise Us," Meadows separates what we observe into three levels. Structure is the source of behavior, and behavior shows itself as a series of events over time. [ch. 4 notes]

Level Example What it lets you do
Event "Payment gateway timed out at 14:02." React. Fix 14:02; learn nothing about 15:30.
Behavior over time "Timeouts have climbed every month-end for six months." Anticipate. Staff up at month-end. Still only adapting.
Structure "Batch settlement and online traffic share one connection pool; month-end volume fills it; clients retry on timeout." Change what the system produces.

A behavior is a graph, not a point: you need at least a few events over time to see one. A structure is made of stocks, flows and feedback loops, plus the rules and information that drive them. Most incident reviews stop at the event level, which is why their explanations sound convincing and their fixes don't last.

Where the sources differ The Iceberg model you'll meet in Module 2 adds a fourth level, mental models, under structure. It isn't Meadows'. Its origin is contested: Innovation Associates, Daniel Kim and Senge are all credited. [Kim]

Linear thinking vs feedback thinking

problem → cause → fix → solved

Linear thinking quietly assumes four things:

  1. There is one cause.
  2. The effect is close to the cause in time and space.
  3. The fix doesn't change the system that made the problem.
  4. The other actors won't react.

Feedback thinking drops all four. Your fix becomes an input, the system responds, and the response changes the problem: 

problem → decision → action → system responds → problem (changed)
   ↑                                                   │
   └───────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Sterman calls the result policy resistance: policies that fail, or worsen the problem they were meant to solve, because of how the system responds. [Sterman 2001] He also argues that "side effects" are just the effects we didn't model: "there are no side effects—just effects." [Sterman 2006]

Senge makes the same points as aphorisms. Three of his eleven laws you will use constantly: [Senge]

  • 1. Today's problems come from yesterday's "solutions."
  • 2. The harder you push, the harder the system pushes back.
  • 7. Cause and effect are not closely related in time and space.
Where the sources differ Senge offers heuristics meant to change how managers think. Sterman insists the structure must be mapped, and ideally simulated, because intuition about dynamics is reliably wrong even among the highly educated. His evidence is the bathtub-dynamics studies, which we'll use in Module 3. Meadows sits between them. In this course, "check your prediction" means Sterman's standard.

Check yourself

Classify each statement. You get feedback immediately.

"Reconciliation exceptions have climbed at every month-end for six months."

Several events read as a trend over time. It tells you what keeps happening, not why.

"Online payments and the nightly settlement batch share one database connection pool, and clients retry on timeout."

It describes connections and a rule (retry on timeout) that generate timeouts. Change these and the behavior changes.

"The settlement batch failed at 02:14 last night."

One point in time. On its own it supports a reaction, nothing more.

"Technicians are measured on on-time aircraft release; nobody tracks whether the same defect returns."

A measurement rule and a missing information flow. These shape what every technician does, whoever is on shift.

"Fraud alerts spiked during the 11.11 sale."

One occurrence. If it happened every 11.11 and 12.12, the series would be a behavior.

"The same hydraulic fitting has been written up on three aircraft in the last 60 days."

A repeat-defect pattern. Your reliability program already works at this level, and the next step is to ask what produces it.

Exercise 1.1: predict first

A Thai digital bank's reconciliation team handles exceptions: transactions where the bank's ledger and the payment network's settlement file disagree. The open-exception count has risen slowly for a quarter. Audit flags it. The ops head responds:

  • New KPI: % of exceptions closed within 24 hours.
  • Each analyst's weekly scorecard shows their closure count.
  • Overdue exceptions are reviewed daily, with the ops head present.

Nothing else changes: same analysts, same upstream systems, same settlement file format.
